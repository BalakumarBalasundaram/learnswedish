# Ordföljd: huvudsats

> 🧠 **Mental model — the verb's reserved seat:** no matter what you put first (subject, time word, adverbial), the finite verb always sits in seat #2. Think of a bus with a reserved seat 2 that the verb never gives up — everything else shuffles around it.

## 📜 Background — the "V2 rule" that English lost

This reserved-seat-2 rule is called **V2 word order**, and it's one of the most distinctive shared features of the Germanic language family — German, Dutch, Norwegian, Danish, and Icelandic all strictly enforce it too. English actually **used to** have it as well (Old English was V2), but lost it gradually during the Middle English period, keeping only small fossil traces (*"Never have I seen such a thing"* — verb jumps to seat 2 only in these leftover poetic/emphatic patterns). So when a Swedish learner says "Idag äter jag" feels backwards compared to English "Today I eat", they're actually seeing the *older*, more conservative Germanic pattern — English is the outlier that drifted away from it.

## Examples

- **Jag** äter äpplet. (I eat the apple.) — subject in seat 1, verb in seat 2
- **Idag** äter jag äpplet. (Today I eat the apple.) — time word in seat 1, verb stays in seat 2
- **Äpplet** äter jag idag. (The apple, I eat today.) — object fronted, verb still in seat 2
